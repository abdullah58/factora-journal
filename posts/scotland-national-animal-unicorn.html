Scotland’s National Animal Is the Unicorn

Scotland’s national animal never existed. Why the unicorn — and what 15th-century heraldry tells us about national myth-making.

By Emilio Lopez · September 19, 2026

Scotland’s national animal is the unicorn: a creature that, to the best of our knowledge, never existed. The choice dates to the 1400s, when Scottish kings adopted the unicorn as a symbol of purity, innocence, and untamable power — the one beast the lion, England’s emblem, could never conquer. It still appears on the Royal Coat of Arms of the United Kingdom, chained on the Scottish side. The symbolism is spelled out in heraldic records going back to James III’s gold unicorn coins.

Medieval bestiaries treated the unicorn as real — a fierce, solitary beast that could only be tamed by a virgin — so to a 15th-century Scot, the emblem was zoology as much as poetry. The unicorn endures on documented national symbols today: passports, government buildings, and the occasional football scarf, a mythical animal doing very real patriotic work.

How we know: Scottish heraldic rolls, surviving unicorn coinage of James III and James IV, and medieval bestiary manuscripts.
