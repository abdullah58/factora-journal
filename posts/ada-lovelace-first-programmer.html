The First Computer Programmer Was a Victorian Countess

A century before computers existed, Ada Lovelace wrote an algorithm for a machine that was never built. The evidence in her 1843 notes.

By Emilio Lopez · September 19, 2026

In 1843, Ada Lovelace published a set of notes on Charles Babbage’s proposed Analytical Engine — a mechanical computer that existed only on paper. Her notes were three times longer than the paper they annotated, and buried in them was a step-by-step method for computing Bernoulli numbers on the Engine: an algorithm, written for a machine that had never been built, a century before the first computer. Her authorship is established in her published notes, archived in full, signed ‘A.A.L.’

Lovelace went further than code. She predicted the Engine might one day compose music and manipulate symbols beyond mere numbers — essentially describing software. Babbage called her ‘the Enchantress of Numbers.’ Her priority is documented in biographical records and the notes themselves, which scholars have verified line by line; the algorithm is correct, and it would have worked.

How we know: Lovelace’s 1843 “Notes” (published with Menabrea’s memoir), Babbage’s correspondence, and modern analyses confirming the algorithm’s validity.
