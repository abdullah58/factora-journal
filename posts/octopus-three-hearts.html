The Animal With Three Hearts and Blue Blood

An octopus has three hearts, blue copper-based blood — and a main heart that stops every time it swims. The biology behind the strangest circulation on Earth.

By Emilio Lopez · September 19, 2026

An octopus carries three hearts: two pump blood to the gills, and one pumps it to the rest of the body. Its blood runs blue, not red, because it uses copper-based hemocyanin instead of iron-based hemoglobin to carry oxygen — a better design for the cold, low-oxygen deep sea. These are not trivia footnotes; they are documented across independently verified species profiles spanning all 300 known octopus species.

Here is the stranger part: the main heart stops beating whenever the octopus swims. Swimming is so exhausting for its body plan that octopuses prefer to crawl along the seafloor — they quite literally get tired faster than almost any animal their size. The whole arrangement, inefficient as it looks, has been confirmed by laboratory observation and dissections going back to the 19th century, and it works: octopuses have thrived for 300 million years.

How we know: cephalopod physiology literature, including laboratory studies of circulation under exertion, and the fossil record of early cephalopods.
