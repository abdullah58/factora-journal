The Day Boston Drowned in Molasses

In 1919, a tank burst in Boston and sent a 25-foot wave of molasses through the North End. The evidence trail behind one of history’s strangest disasters.

By Emilio Lopez · September 19, 2026

On January 15, 1919, a riveted steel tank in Boston’s North End split open and released 2.3 million gallons of molasses. Witnesses described a 25-foot wave moving at 35 miles per hour — fast enough to rip buildings from foundations, flip a truck, and tear an elevated railway girder from its moorings. Twenty-one people died and 150 were injured, making it one of the best-documented industrial disasters on record. For decades, Bostonians swore you could still smell molasses on hot summer days.

The tank had been filled to capacity and poorly built — it leaked from the day it was commissioned, and the company painted it brown to hide the seepage. A rapid temperature swing, from bitter cold to 40°F, plus fermenting molasses building gas pressure inside, finished the job. The full sequence was reconstructed from court transcripts and engineering reports filed during the landmark lawsuit that followed, one of the first to use expert scientific testimony in an American courtroom.

How we know: trial records from the 1920–25 lawsuit, contemporary newspaper coverage, and a 2016 Harvard fluid-dynamics study that modeled the wave’s speed.
