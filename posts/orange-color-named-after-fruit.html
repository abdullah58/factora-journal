Oranges Weren’t Orange — the Color Was Named After the Fruit

Before the 1500s, English had no word for the color orange — and the original oranges were green. The etymology and botany behind the name.

By Emilio Lopez · September 19, 2026

The word ‘orange’ described the fruit centuries before it described the color. The first recorded English use of ‘orange’ for the hue dates to around 1512; before that, English speakers called the color ‘yellow-red’ (ġeolurēad). The fruit arrived first, via Persian nāranj and Arabic nāranj, and the color simply borrowed its name. The trail is clear in etymological records including the Oxford English Dictionary’s dated quotations.

And the original oranges weren’t orange at all. Wild and early cultivated oranges in Southeast Asia were green — and in tropical regions they still are, because warm nights let chlorophyll linger in the rind. The familiar bright-orange skin is partly a cool-climate effect, partly centuries of selective breeding. It’s all laid out in botanical documentation of citrus cultivation: the fruit named the color, then humans recolored the fruit to match.

How we know: OED etymology entries for “orange”; botanical literature on citrus rind coloration and climate.
