The Physicist Who Was Told Hot Water Can’t Freeze Faster

A Tanzanian student noticed hot ice-cream mix froze faster than cold. He was laughed at — until a physicist tested it. The strange, still-debated Mpemba effect.

By Emilio Lopez · September 19, 2026

In 1963, Tanzanian schoolboy Erasto Mpemba was making ice cream in class when he noticed something wrong: his hot mix froze before his classmates’ cooled mix. His teacher mocked him. Undeterred, Mpemba asked a visiting physicist, Denis Osborne, who tested it in his lab and got the same result. Under certain conditions, hot water really can freeze faster than cold — and the phenomenon now carries the student’s name in published experimental records beginning with Mpemba and Osborne’s 1969 paper.

Here’s the twist that makes it a factora story: nobody fully agrees on why. Candidates include faster evaporation of hot water, stronger convection currents, fewer dissolved gases, and differences in supercooling. Aristotle and Descartes both noticed something similar centuries earlier, yet modern labs still argue about the mechanism — a fact that survives in independently replicated studies while its explanation remains gloriously unsettled.

How we know: Mpemba & Osborne (1969), plus dozens of replication attempts catalogued in the physics literature — including several that failed, which is itself informative.
